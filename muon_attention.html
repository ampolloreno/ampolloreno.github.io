Thoughts on Muon and Second Order Optimization

Muon has a clean derivation for linear layers. For a linear layer \(y = xW\), the steepest first-order update under a spectral-norm constraint is the polar factor of the gradient. If \(G = \nabla_W L\), then

\[ \Delta W \propto -\operatorname{Polar}(G) = -UV^\top, \qquad G = U\Sigma V^\top. \]

Muon approximates this polar factor with Newton–Schulz iterations. That derivation is fine for linear maps, but attention changes the constrained object. For one attention head, \(Q = XW_Q\), \(K = XW_K\), and \(V = XW_V\). The attention logits are \(S = \frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt d} = \frac{XW_QW_K^\top X^\top}{\sqrt d}\).

The object that directly controls attention is not \(W_Q\) or \(W_K\) separately. It is the bilinear product \(W_QW_K^\top\). If one orthogonalizes the updates to \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\) independently, one is still applying the linear-layer derivation. Attention does not use \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\) independently. It uses their product. For attention, the relevant constraint belongs on the induced change in \(QK^\top\).

Empirical picture

Large-scale Muon training already shows this mismatch. Moonshot’s Muon scaling report identifies two crucial fixes for scaling Muon, weight decay and per-matrix update-RMS rescaling. It also notes that Muon’s update RMS depends on matrix shape, which can become problematic for small attention matrices such as per-head KV matrices in GQA/MLA-style attention. In their training dynamics, the maximum attention logit can rise above 100 in some layers, even when loss and gradient norm remain stable. [Liu et al., 2025]

Kimi K2 introduces MuonClip, whose main new ingredient is QK-Clip. The paper says QK-Clip rescales query and key projection weights after the optimizer step to explicitly constrain attention logits. Muon alone is not enough for attention. [Kimi Team, 2025] DeepSeek-V4 takes the architectural route. It uses Muon for most matrix parameters, but applies RMSNorm to attention queries and compressed KV entries immediately before core attention. The paper attributes that normalization to logit stability and does not use QK-Clip. [DeepSeek-AI, 2026] Across these reports, Muon is used for matrix parameters and attention receives extra QK/logit control. That is the expected outcome when the linear-layer derivation is applied to a bilinear operator.

Attention-space derivation

For a linear layer, the idealized Muon problem is

\[ \min_{\Delta W}\; \langle G, \Delta W \rangle \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \|\Delta W\|_2 \le \rho. \]

For attention, the analogous constraint should not be on \(\Delta W_Q\) and \(\Delta W_K\) separately. It should be on the induced change in the attention logits.

For one head, \(S = \frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt d}\). A first-order perturbation gives

\[ \Delta S = \frac{(X\Delta W_Q)K^\top + Q(X\Delta W_K)^\top}{\sqrt d}. \]

So the attention-aware Muon problem is

\[ \min_{\Delta W_Q,\Delta W_K} \langle G_Q,\Delta W_Q\rangle + \langle G_K,\Delta W_K\rangle \]

subject to

\[ \left\| \frac{(X\Delta W_Q)K^\top + Q(X\Delta W_K)^\top}{\sqrt d} \right\| \le \rho. \]

If we write \(\theta=(W_Q,W_K)\), \(g=\nabla_\theta L\), and \(J_S\) for the Jacobian mapping parameter updates to logit updates, then the exact trust-region update has the natural-gradient/Gauss–Newton form

\[ \Delta\theta^* = -\rho\, \frac{(J_S^\top J_S + \lambda I)^{-1}g} {\sqrt{g^\top (J_S^\top J_S + \lambda I)^{-1}g}}. \]

The update couples \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\) because logit changes couple them. The step should be judged by the induced change in \(QK^\top\), not by the two matrices separately.

Optimizer-side implementation

Muon is cheap because it is local to one matrix. You take a matrix-shaped momentum buffer, run a few Newton–Schulz iterations, rescale the update, and apply it. The attention-aware update is not local to one matrix. It depends on the current activations \(X\), the current queries \(Q\), the current keys \(K\), the attention mask, the head structure, the sequence length, the softmax geometry, and in modern models compressed, sparse, latent, grouped, or multi-query attention layouts. Even before considering the softmax, the natural object is the logit matrix \(S\), which is sequence-by-sequence. In dense attention this is \(T \times T\) per head. In sparse or compressed attention, it becomes architecture-specific. In MLA-style or compressed-KV attention, the key may not even be materialized in the same way at training and inference. The exact optimizer-side solution is expensive. The architecture is the cheaper place to impose the same geometry.

QK normalization

The scalar attention logit is \(\ell(q,k) = \frac{q^\top k}{\sqrt d}\). The dangerous degrees of freedom are the radial norms of \(q\) and \(k\). If Muon updates \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\) independently, the product \(QK^\top\) can grow even when the individual matrix updates look reasonable. A direct architectural fix is to set \(\hat q = R_q \frac{q}{\|q\|_2 + \epsilon}\) and \(\hat k = R_k \frac{k}{\|k\|_2 + \epsilon}\), and then compute \(\ell(q,k)=\frac{\hat q^\top \hat k}{\sqrt d}\). In transformer notation this becomes \(\hat Q_h = \operatorname{RMSNorm}(Q_h)\) and \(\hat K_h = \operatorname{RMSNorm}(K_h)\), followed by \(S_h = \alpha_h\frac{\hat Q_h\hat K_h^\top}{\sqrt d}\).

The scalar \(\alpha_h\) sets a per-head temperature or radius budget. One scalar per head matches the isotropic version of the argument. A full RMSNorm gamma vector learns a diagonal metric instead.

Now the logits are bounded. In particular, \(|\ell(q,k)| \le \frac{\|\hat q\|_2\|\hat k\|_2}{\sqrt d} = \frac{R_qR_k}{\sqrt d}\). Even more importantly, the local sensitivity is bounded. Here \(\nabla_{\hat q}\ell = \frac{\hat k}{\sqrt d}\) and \(\nabla_{\hat k}\ell = \frac{\hat q}{\sqrt d}\), so \(\|\nabla_{\hat q}\ell\|_2 = \frac{R_k}{\sqrt d}\) and \(\|\nabla_{\hat k}\ell\|_2 = \frac{R_q}{\sqrt d}\). This matches the operator control Muon gives a linear map.

The Jacobian view

Take exact L2 normalization, \(\hat q = R\frac{q}{\|q\|_2}\), and write \(u = \frac{q}{\|q\|_2}\). Then

\[ D\hat q = \frac{R}{\|q\|_2} \left(I - uu^\top\right). \]

The term \(I - uu^\top\) is the projection onto the tangent space of the sphere. QK norm removes the radial direction. An update that merely increases \(\|q\|\) does not change the attention logits. Under normalization, the optimizer no longer has to learn not to spend update budget on radial Q/K growth. The forward map removes that direction directly.

Attention-aware trust regions and QK normalization address the same instability at different levels. One constrains the update in logit space. The other changes the parameterization so the unstable radial direction is mostly absent. This is also the role of DeepSeek-V4’s pre-attention normalization. Muon remains matrix-local, and the model removes the unstable radial Q/K direction before attention.

Choice of norm

The Muon derivation points to per-head L2/RMS normalization on Q and K. Muon’s motivating geometry is spectral norm. Spectral norm is the operator norm induced by Euclidean vector norms, namely \(\|W\|_2 = \sup_{\|x\|_2=1}\|Wx\|_2\).

The vector geometry paired with this is \(\ell_2\). Equivalently, to bound the dot product, Holder’s inequality gives \(|q^\top k| \le \|q\|_p\|k\|_{p^*}\) with \(\frac1p + \frac1{p^*}=1\). The only symmetric self-dual choice is \(p=2\).

It is also the only rotation-invariant choice. That matters because Muon’s polar update is built from SVD/orthogonal geometry. An \(\ell_1\) or \(\ell_\infty\) constraint picks out coordinate axes. It is not the same geometry. This points to per-head RMS/L2 normalization on Q and K before the dot product. RMSNorm is just L2 normalization written in the usual transformer scale, since \(\operatorname{RMS}(q)=\frac{\|q\|_2}{\sqrt d}\).

Scalar softcaps

A scalar cap such as \(\ell \mapsto \tau\tanh(\ell/\tau)\) or \(\ell \mapsto \tau\arctan(\ell/\tau)\) bounds the logit value after the dot product. That bounds values after the dot product. It does not bound the operator generating them. If \(c(\ell)\) is the scalar cap, then \(\nabla_q c(\ell) = c'(\ell)\frac{k}{\sqrt d}\).

Near \(\ell=0\), most softcaps have \(c'(0)\approx 1\). Therefore, if \(k\) has huge norm but happens to be nearly orthogonal to \(q\), the scalar logit is small while the local sensitivity can still be huge. A softcap controls values. QK norm controls the operator.

Under the Muon/operator-norm story, QK RMSNorm is the architectural match. A scalar cap can still serve as a guardrail, but it does not fix the missing QK geometry.

Three placements of the same constraint

Kimi K2 applies the constraint after the optimizer step through QK-Clip. An attention-aware Muon would impose it in the update itself. DeepSeek-V4 moves it into the forward pass with QK normalization. The underlying object is the same in each case: control the change in \(QK^\top\).

Muon and optimizer geometry

Muon is not a cheap Hessian inverse. It is a structured matrix update. Adam-style methods use diagonal gradient statistics. Muon uses matrix geometry and a controlled spectral profile. This is the setting where maximal-update arguments apply. Shah et al. study Muon together with maximal update parameterization and find that Muon expands the compute-time Pareto frontier over AdamW while retaining data efficiency at large batch sizes. [Shah et al., 2025]

Moonshot’s Muon report gives a similar empirical result. After adding weight decay and correcting update RMS, their scaling-law experiments show roughly a 2x compute-efficiency improvement over AdamW under their compute-optimal setup. [Liu et al., 2025] But the attention story says that matrix-level maximal updates are not enough. For MLP matrices, the matrix is the operator. For attention Q/K, the operator is the product. Use Muon where the parameter is the operator. Add QK geometry where the operator is bilinear.

Batch-size implications

Preconditioning changes the usual critical-batch calculation. In the first-order setting, the expected progress has the familiar diminishing-returns form

\[ \Delta L_{\mathrm{opt}}(B) = \frac{\Delta L_{\max}}{1+B_{\mathrm{noise}}/B}. \]

The noise scale predicts the point where increasing batch size gives diminishing returns. If we add a fixed deterministic preconditioner \(P\), the same calculation gives a modified critical batch size

\[ B_P = \frac{\operatorname{tr}(HP\Sigma P)}{g^\top P H P g}. \]

For Newton or natural-gradient style preconditioning with \(P=H^{-1}\), this becomes

\[ B_N = \frac{\operatorname{tr}(H^{-1}\Sigma)}{g^\top H^{-1}g}. \]

So even before stochastic curvature estimation, preconditioning changes the noise metric. The relevant gradient noise is now whitened by curvature. If the curvature itself is estimated stochastically, there is a second statistical problem. The update is no longer just \(H^{-1}\hat g\). It is \(\hat H^{-1}\hat g\). That creates two noise scales. One is a gradient-side scale and the other is a curvature-side scale. In the notation of my note,

\[ B_g = \frac{\operatorname{tr}(H^{-1}\Sigma_g)}{g^\top H^{-1}g}, \]

and

\[ B_h = \frac{u^\top \mathbb E[\delta H^{-1}\delta]u}{g^\top H^{-1}g}, \qquad u=H^{-1}g. \]

The second-order progress law is approximately

\[ \frac{\Delta L_{\mathrm{opt}}(B_1,B_2)}{\Delta L^N_{\max}} \approx \frac{1}{1+B_g/B_1+B_h/B_2}. \]

So second-order optimization has a critical surface, not a single critical batch size.

\[ \frac{B_g}{B_1}+\frac{B_h}{B_2}\approx 1. \]

For full stochastic curvature estimation there are therefore two batch scales, one for gradients and one for curvature.

But practical Muon is not full stochastic Newton. It does not draw a separate curvature minibatch and invert it. It uses a strong structural prior. The update should be matrix-orthogonalized. It also uses temporal averaging through momentum.

If an estimator is exponentially averaged as \(s_t=(1-\beta)x_t+\beta s_{t-1}\), then under stationarity its variance is reduced by \(\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}\). The effective sample multiplier is therefore \(M_\beta = \frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}\). For \(\beta=0.95\), \(M_\beta \approx 39\). For \(\beta=0.99\), \(M_\beta \approx 199\).

Matrix-whitening optimizers can therefore behave more second-order without requiring a huge separate curvature batch. They combine structure and averaging. Second-order-ish optimizers change the statistical estimation problem.

For full curvature, the curvature-side batch can be enormous. For structured or factorized curvature, it can be much smaller. In the critical-curvature note, full outer-product curvature has \(B_h=d+1\) in a whitened Gaussian block, while a K-FAC block of shape \(n\times m\) has

\[ B_h^{\mathrm{KFAC}} \approx n+m+2. \]

The same lesson appears here. Structure matters. Scalable second-order behavior does not come from estimating a giant dense curvature matrix. It comes from choosing a structured geometry with a smaller statistical burden.

This is consistent with the empirical Muon story. Public dense training batches are still largely governed by the gradient-side critical batch and hardware utilization. The second-order side can be kept under control by structure, momentum, and update-scale normalization.

Spectral-norm sanity check

There is also a simple stability reason to like Muon. For a linear layer, if the Muon update is exactly polar-normalized, then \(\|\operatorname{Polar}(G)\|_2 = 1\). So the operator norm of the raw update is controlled, with \(\|\Delta W\|_2 \le \eta\rho\). This does not prove that training is globally stable. But it does say that the per-step perturbation of the layer as an operator is bounded in the geometry that the layer actually uses.

For attention, the analogous bound would be on \(\Delta(W_QW_K^\top)\). Using first-order expansion,

\[ \Delta(W_QW_K^\top) \approx \Delta W_Q W_K^\top + W_Q\Delta W_K^\top. \]

Independent bounds on \(\Delta W_Q\) and \(\Delta W_K\) do not directly bound the product in the same clean way unless we also control the norms of \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\) or normalize the resulting queries and keys. Again, QK norm is exactly the architecture-side fix. If \(q,k \mapsto \hat q,\hat k\), then \(|\hat q^\top\hat k| \le \|\hat q\|_2\|\hat k\|_2\). The spectral story and the attention story match.

Practical implications

A Muon transformer should treat these pieces separately. Use Muon on parameters whose forward role is linear, such as MLP matrices, expert matrices, dense projections, routers, and possibly value/output projections. Treat Q/K separately with per-head RMS/L2 normalization before the dot product. Use QK-Clip only as a guardrail when the architecture makes direct normalization awkward. Log max logit, RMS logit, large-logit ratio, query/key RMS, and update RMS by matrix shape. Estimate critical batch size at small scale for the actual optimizer rather than importing an SGD schedule.

Muon gives a principled matrix update for linear operators. Attention depends on the bilinear operator \(QK^\top\), so the same derivation does not apply unchanged. QK normalization is the simplest way to restore that missing geometry in the model.
